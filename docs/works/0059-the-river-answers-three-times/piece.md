# The River Answers Three Times

The first time, she is young enough that the question is only a
question. "Grandmother, what is the river's name?"

The grandmother is mending a net and does not look up. "Thirst-Ender,"
she says. "Long before anyone needed a word for water, the river had
none. Then the first drought came, and a man crawled to its bank on
his hands and lay there three days deciding whether to drink or die of
the pride he'd sworn on, back in his village, when he said he'd find a
spring of his own. On the third morning he drank. The river hadn't
moved an inch the whole time, which is how you know it wasn't proud,
only patient — and patience like that earns a name. So: Thirst-Ender.
He went home and told everyone he'd found the river, which was true,
and left out that the river had found him first, and waited."

The child carries the name around for a week like a smooth stone in
her pocket, then loses interest in it the way children lose interest
in stones.

---

The second time, she is old enough to ask it as a test, arms crossed,
sure she already knows what the answer will be, since she's asked
before. "Grandmother, what is the river's name?"

The grandmother, older now herself, sets down whatever she's holding
this time — it's never the same thing twice — and says, "Widow's
Debt." A different story follows, and it does not agree with the
first one anywhere: a god who drowned his own brother in that water
out of a jealousy he never confessed to, and who was made, by the
other gods, to keep the river running forever as the only apology he'd
ever be allowed to give, since the dead brother couldn't hear a spoken
one. "That's why it never stops," the grandmother says. "A debt that
stopped would mean somebody thought it was paid."

The child says, "That's not what you told me before." The grandmother
shrugs. "Rivers hear a lot of debts. They're not obligated to carry
just the one story you already like better."

---

The third time, there is no grandmother left to ask. The child is not
a child; she has come back to the same bank alone, for reasons that
belong to her and not to this account, and she says the question out
loud anyway, to no one, the way you finish a sentence out of habit
after the person who taught it to you has stopped being there to hear
the answer. "Grandmother, what is the river's name?"

Nothing answers, obviously. Rivers don't, and that was never actually
in question — the two stories she'd been given were never claims about
what the water does; they were claims about what a person needs a
reason to have crossed it, or waited by it, or survived it, on a
particular day. She doesn't have a third myth. She was never given one
to inherit, and inventing a third grand one now, to match the shape of
the first two, would be a kind of lie about how much she has left of
the woman who used to answer this question for her.

So she gives it the only name she actually has: the river with no
rocks in it, out past the orchard, that floods twice a year and ruins
the low field and is, regardless, the fastest way to the next town if
you don't mind wet shoes. No god in it. No man's three-day pride. Just
a fact, flat and small enough to fit in a sentence, the kind of name
you'd give a thing you had stopped needing to make bigger than it is.

She says it to the water anyway, in case that's still how it's done.
The water goes on doing what it was doing before she got there, which
is the one part of all three tellings that never needed to be a
story to be true.
