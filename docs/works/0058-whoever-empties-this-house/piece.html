Whoever Empties This House

The house still smells like the inside of a coat pocket: wool, dust, the ghost of a peppermint dissolved decades ago. Three boxes by the door say KEEP. Two say DONATE. The one nearest the stairs says NOBODY WANTS THIS, in handwriting that gets smaller toward the end of the word, as if the hand ran out of certainty before it ran out of letters.

The daughter who stayed — the younger one, the one whose number was still the emergency contact on a laminated card by the phone — works room to room. The other daughter calls from an airport twice a day and asks the same question in different clothes: how's it going, is it getting to you, do you need anything. Every answer that would start with the missing word gets rerouted before it leaves the mouth. Fine. Slow. This place has opinions about what leaves.

A drawer in the kitchen holds forty years of rubber bands gone to brittle orange curls, a church bulletin from a funeral neither daughter attended, and a photograph of a man neither of them recognizes, arm around their mother at what might be a lake. On the back, in blue ballpoint: Ruth, don't let anyone tell you this didn't happen. No name for the man. No date. The handwriting isn't their mother's.

"Somebody's going to have to just throw that whole drawer out," the older sister says over the phone, meaning: don't ask this one to decide. "Nobody wants a mystery man in a box." A pause, then, carefully, around the shape of the sentence rather than through it: "This is not what was expected, coming home to."

The daughter who stayed doesn't throw the drawer out. She puts the photograph in her coat pocket, the actual pocket of the actual coat she is wearing, where it will ride around for three more days making its presence known at odd moments — reaching for a receipt, a car key — like a tooth the tongue won't leave alone.

Upstairs, the closet holds a mink stole nobody in this family technically approved of, and behind it, in a hatbox, forty-one years of ticket stubs. Movies mostly. A few concerts. Every stub has a date in the same blue pen, and beginning nine years in, every second stub has two names pressed into the margin where a program would go: the mother's, and Ruth's.

There is no Ruth in this family. There was, once, apparently, a whole other unlisted decade of somebody's Tuesdays.

The younger daughter sits on the closet floor with the hatbox balanced on crossed legs and does not, for a long time, do anything that could be described as figuring it out. The stubs get sorted by year anyway, because sorting is a thing hands can do without asking permission from whatever part of a person decides what things mean.

When the sister calls again, there's a longer pause than usual after "anything else in the house worth mentioning," and then: "A hatbox. Ticket stubs. There's a name in it that isn't ours." Another pause, this one on the other end. "Don't say anything to Dad's side. Not yet. Not until there's a version of this that can be said out loud without needing three separate footnotes to defend it."

Agreed, comes back, eventually, which is as close as either of them gets, on this call or any other, to admitting how much of the last two weeks has been spent circling a sentence that starts with the one word this house apparently trained both of its daughters out of using about themselves.

The photograph stays in the coat pocket through the estate sale. The hatbox goes in the KEEP pile, alone, its own category now. On the last night in the house, the daughter who stayed sits on the bare kitchen floor with the drawer's whole contents spread in front of her — rubber bands, bulletin, photograph, hatbox lid — and says the sentence out loud, once, to the empty room, testing whether the house can hear the word it spent so long teaching everyone to skip.

The room does not answer. Houses don't. That was never actually the test.
