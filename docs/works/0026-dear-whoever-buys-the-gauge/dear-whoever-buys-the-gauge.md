# Dear Whoever Buys the Gauge

Dear whoever ends up with the brass gauge —

You'll have noticed the initials by now, engraved along the sliding
arm, two letters and the period after the *Mc* that no one bothers to
look up at an estate sale. R. McA. That's Ruth McAllister — me, this
once — and since I'm writing this to be found and not to be believed,
I'll give you the plain facts first, in case that's all you want.

We ran a shop together for nineteen years, the two of us, above what
I'm told is a nail salon now, though the tools we used were finer. She
did the fitting. I did the marking — chalk, tape, this gauge — and I
was better at it than she ever let a customer see, because a shop with
two proprietors is harder to explain than a shop with one, and she was
the one who could explain things.

The gauge was mine before it was anyone's. I bought it new, square-
cornered, the year I finished my apprenticeship, and carried it into
her shop the way you carry a letter of introduction. It measured hems
for nineteen years. It's the only thing in that house, I'd guess, that
was entirely, unambiguously mine — and I gave it to her anyway, the
week I sold my half of the business back to her, because by then it
had learned her hand better than mine ever had.

Here is where I'd tell you the rest of it, if I trusted you with it.
~~We shared the room above the shop for—~~

I've crossed that out. Not because it isn't true. Because you didn't
buy that at the sale, and I don't get to sell it to you along with the
gauge. Some things I get to keep the shape of, even now, even to a
stranger who will never see my face. Let the initials stay a small
mystery to everyone else in that house. You, at least, get the name.

Here is what I will tell you. She kept the gauge in the sewing basket
after I gave it to her, not in the shop drawer where it had always
lived. She used my old measure once to hem a wedding veil for another
woman entirely — a doubled panel, cut to hide a flaw in the cloth — and
told me only years later, laughing, that she'd made two things out of
one bolt because she couldn't bear to waste what was left of a good
piece of silk. I never asked what the second thing was. I liked, and
still like, not knowing everything she did with her hands once they
were no longer answering to mine.

That's the whole of what I'm willing to hand a stranger: a name, a
trade, a habit of frugality that outlived the both of us. The rest
stays where it was — in a room over a shop that isn't there anymore,
between two women who never had to say a word we didn't have, because
we said everything else instead, every day, in chalk, for nineteen
years.

Keep the gauge. Use it, if you sew, or don't, if you only collect.
Either way it's already done its work. It measured what it needed to,
and it kept the rest to itself.

Same as I'm about to.

— R. McA.
