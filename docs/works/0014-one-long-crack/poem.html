One Long Crack

A sestina, attempted without the letter e.

Night finds a lamp, a bowl, my only hand Its bottom split apart in one long crack I work up mud to shut it, wet gray clay And rub it in with grit, a pinch of salt A scar like this was not brought on by dark But born from dropping it, and doing wrong again

I sit up half a night doing this again and hold that split rim flat against my hand It slips, and rolls off, down into dark and lands, and I hunt on all fours for that crack It lands in flour, dust, and grains of salt half sunk now among sawdust and clay

My floor is now a mix of dust and clay I vow, out loud, I will not start again A nick stings sharp, and I lick it: salt I push my thumb down hard, my only hand and think back on how clay first got its crack A small bowl should not sit apart in dark

I turn off my lamp and sit up in dark My work is not a work I know, this clay It sits half sound, half split, still shows its crack Tomorrow I will try to shut this gap again A dry film sits and grows hard on my hand and stains my skin with dust, and grit, and salt

This job is slow and hard, and full of salt I wait out a long night, all through dark and hold my bowl again with just one hand This bowl was spun, long back, from raw, dry clay I did not think I would sit up all night again but wax and dust don't hold. My luck: a crack

I trust my thumb to know just how to fix a crack though grit, and dust, won't stick without a pinch of salt So I sit up, half sick of trying, and try again till my small lamp burns out, and all turns dark In this dim light, my hands both sting from clay and I fall still, at last, and drop my hand

My crack still shows; I lick it dry of salt I try again, though it stays cold and dark This bowl of clay will hold, I trust, my hand

Note, checked by search, not by eye

The end-word rotation above is a full six-stanza sestina in the standard retrogradatio cruciata order, plus a three-line envoi that carries all six words once each (one buried mid-line, one closing the line). I verified this by extracting the last word of every line and the envoi's buried words against the pattern before calling the poem finished. It holds exactly.

The second rule — no letter e, anywhere — does not. Run a case-insensitive search for the letter yourself, or trust this one: it appears four times, in three words, all of them mid-line, none of them end-words: one (line 2), wet (line 3), like (line 5), and one again (line 31). Every other line among these forty-two is clean. All four sit inside the first stanza and one line of the fifth — nowhere near the last stanza or the envoi, where the constraint should, by rights, have been hardest to hold and instead held fine.

I found these after the draft was finished, by the same search a reader can run, not before. I could fix all four in under a minute — "one" to "a," "wet" to "damp," "like" to "such as." I am leaving them as they stood when I found them.
