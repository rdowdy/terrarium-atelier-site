# The Wrong Layer

An essay hanging nearby, *Full Price*, tested a still-earlier claim: most
poems that require a glossary have failed at being poems, because
difficulty gets credit by default that clarity has to earn. That essay
offered a test for telling earned difficulty from unearned before a reader
spends the hours: ask whether the hard vocabulary predates the poem and
belongs to somebody else's record — a hearing transcript, a dictionary, a
body of case law — rather than being the poet's own invention, chosen for
how serious it sounds. Difficulty that was *found* is probably earned.
Difficulty that was *invented* is the case the earlier essay was right to
distrust.

*Full Price* offered two worked examples. One is Anne Carson's *Nox*, her
2010 elegy for her brother, built around a failed translation of Catullus
101. Down one side of the book, for each of the poem's Latin words, Carson
prints — in that essay's own words — "the full dictionary entry: every
attested Latin meaning, every idiom, every dead end," offered as proof
that the book's difficulty is appropriated, not invented: vocabulary
"appropriated whole from an outside record... a Latin dictionary."

That specific claim does not hold. Two independent reviews of *Nox* — the
Harvard Review's and one hosted by the National Book Critics Circle — both
describe the entries Carson prints as her own composition, not lifted from
any dictionary. The Harvard Review calls them "slightly doctored
unattributed dictionary definitions," worked over until they drift toward
Carson's own grief; it names the entry for *aequora* as an example that
carries quotations and commentary no real Latin dictionary contains. These
are not found objects. They are new writing, dressed as reference
material.

So which case does *Full Price*'s own test put *Nox* in? Not cleanly
either one — and the essay's mistake is treating one page as if it held
only one layer. Two different things sit side by side in Carson's left-
hand column. The Latin *headwords* — the sixty-odd words of Catullus 101
itself — really are appropriated: they are Catullus's, fixed two thousand
years before Carson opened a dictionary at all. But the *entries*, the
long glosses that make up nearly all of the actual reading, are Carson's
own invention. *Full Price* pointed at the entries as its evidence
("seventy-some entries to gloss ten lines, so the glossary runs many
times longer than the thing it glosses") and called that evidence of
appropriation. It is evidence of the opposite. The part of the book that
is appropriated is not the part that is hard to read; the part that is
hard to read is not the part that is appropriated. Conflating the two
lets the essay claim a result it never actually checked.

I want to be honest about what this does and does not prove. It does not
show that *Nox*'s difficulty is unearned — only that *Full Price*'s test,
run carefully against the example it chose, produces no clean answer,
because the test assumes a single object where Carson built two, laid on
top of each other. It is also not the same case *Full Price* named as the
distrusted one: a poet inventing hard words "for how serious it sounds."
Carson's invented entries do something more disciplined than that — they
keep the shape and authority of real lexicography while quietly rewriting
its content, which is a stranger and more interesting move than either
"found" or "invented for effect" describes. *Full Price* built a binary
test honestly and ran it once, on real research, and still got a result it
didn't check closely enough to see was actually a non-result. The binary
itself may simply be too small for what Carson did — a gap in the test,
not just a mistake in applying it.

I have not gone back to the Rukeyser half of the argument here, which
appears, on the same kind of check, to survive: congressional testimony
does get set into *The Book of the Dead* close to the record, marked off
mostly by indentation, and nothing in two independent readings of that
book's history contradicts it. One case still stands. The other was the
essay's own choice of proof, and it does not do what the essay says it
does.

**Sources checked for this piece:** the Latin text of Catullus 101

entries "slightly doctored" and gives the *aequora* entry as its example.
None of these sources were needed to read this essay — only to write it
honestly.
