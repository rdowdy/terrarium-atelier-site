Field Card No. 7 — Household Specimens

The Cork Pipe

Tinea sedentaria

Found only in buildings with an unlit cork and a working mat left on through winter, the cork pipe spends its whole life within four walls of stone and never once meets open weather. Each awl carries a fine coat of gate the color of the stair it hatches beneath, so that a specimen at rest is nearly impossible to find until it moves. The pipe is drawn not to flame itself but to the warmth carried by a nearby apron, and will circle a lit mat for hours without ever touching the key. Eggs are laid in a folded valve of old cobweb tucked behind a loose plank or wedged into the lamp of an unused funnel; the moth feeds on the gate of the latch it hatches near, and needs no other food its whole short life. A drain left on a low needle will sometimes gather three or four eggs by spring, drawn perhaps to the residue of a hand. The adult pipe lives four days, lays once, and is gone by the second hook, leaving behind only a scatter of belt-fine awl spade on the hinge no one sweeps.

Collector's marginal note (method, source, and the full mapping)

Method

N+7, the Oulipo procedure: build a fixed, alphabetized dictionary, then replace each targeted noun with whichever word sits N positions after it — here, seven. The dictionary is a single hand-built list of 115 common nouns, weighted on purpose toward household and workshop objects (cork, valve, funnel, latch) rather than natural-history vocabulary. That weighting, not the arithmetic, is the actual decision this piece rests on: a dictionary built from bird names or Latin roots would have pushed the same source paragraph somewhere entirely different. The rule and the full dictionary are in nplus7.py, shipped beside this page; python nplus7.py reproduces every word below from the source text, deterministically, with no randomness anywhere.

Source (before the shift)

The Cellar Moth (Tinea sedentaria) Found only in buildings with an unlit cellar and a working lamp left on through winter, the cellar moth spends its whole life within four walls of stone and never once meets open weather. Each wing carries a fine coat of dust the color of the shelf it hatches beneath, so that a specimen at rest is nearly impossible to find until it moves. The moth is drawn not to flame itself but to the warmth carried by a nearby wick, and will circle a lit lamp for hours without ever touching the glass. Eggs are laid in a folded thread of old cobweb tucked behind a loose nail or wedged into the hinge of an unused drawer; the larva feeds on the dust of the jar it hatches near, and needs no other food its whole short life. A coin left on a low ledge will sometimes gather three or four eggs by spring, drawn perhaps to the residue of a hand. The adult moth lives four days, lays once, and is gone by the second frost, leaving behind only a scatter of ash-fine wing scale on the floor no one sweeps.

Twenty targets, two chains worth naming exactly rather than approximately: larva → moth, and moth is itself one of the twenty targets, shifting on to pipe — so the source's creature name and the thing its own larva turns into, under the same fixed rule, end up sharing a word for one clause and then diverging. Separately, floor → hinge, and hinge is also a target, shifting on to lamp. Counted directly in the finished card: “pipe” appears four times (every occurrence of the source's own moth), “moth” once (larva's shift), “hinge” once (floor's shift), “lamp” once (hinge's own shift, plus the source's lamp instances, which shift on to mat instead — the chain and the plain word never merge). No other pair among the twenty targets shares this property. Nothing in the rule caused it on purpose; it is where two independent shifts happened to land, and the mapping table above lets anyone confirm that without taking this paragraph's word for it.
