Four Hands on One Frame

A poem in five parts.

I. Grozing

She works the diamond cutter along the same line her grandmother scored forty years ago, a hairline neither of them meant to be permanent. The cames soften under her thumb — old lead, gone soft as beeswax, easy to bend, easier to break. She grozes the last quarry free of its bed and the whole pane comes loose at once, the way a held breath comes loose, not gradually. Fire-polished on one edge only; she never finished this one. Nobody told her she wouldn't get to.

II. Encumbrance

Everything she owned converts, on paper, to a number that means nothing about her. The house: appraised, encumbered by a lien nobody remembers taking out. Chain of title, unbroken since 1961, about to break for the first time in a signature. Quit-claim, easement, escheat should no claim be filed — the words exist for exactly this, a machinery built to move a life sideways into other hands without saying where it went. He fills in the boxes. He does not cry at the boxes. He cries later, over something smaller.

III. Transition

I have caught babies and I know what this is: the hardest part comes right before the letting go, not after — they call it transition and nobody warns you it looks like dying. Crowning is a word for both directions; something comes to the edge of the body and either enters the world or leaves it, and from where I stood, holding her hand, I could not for a moment tell which one this was. No caul over this one. No cry. Just the lightening after, the room suddenly lighter by the exact weight of her.

IV. Occultation

Jupiter went behind the moon at 4:12 that afternoon — I'd set the telescope up days before, not for this. An occultation is just one body passing in front of another, timed to the second, predictable a hundred years out, and still, when it happens, you can't quite believe the sky obeyed the almanac. Syzygy: three things in one line. I stood in the yard and did not go in. Somebody has to keep time from the outside. Somebody has to say it happened at 4:12 exactly, because inside no one was looking at a clock.

V. Chord

The pane comes loose the way a held breath comes loose. The chain of title breaks for the first time in a signature. Crowning is a word for both directions. Jupiter went behind the moon at 4:12 exactly.

Glossary

Read each section once for the trade, once for the afternoon. Every term below belongs only to the section it's listed under — no section borrows another's vocabulary. That's the checkable part of the claim: the words are real, used correctly, and they were never doing double duty by accident.

I — Glazier (granddaughter)

  • came — a slim lead strip that holds each piece of glass in a leaded window. Also: what held the family together, and is now soft enough to bend.
  • quarry — one small diamond-shaped pane within a leaded window, not a place. Also: the smallest piece she can still hold onto.
  • groze — to nibble or trim a glass edge to its final shape with a grozing tool, rather than cutting it clean in one stroke. Also: the slow, imprecise way a life gets shaped down to what's kept.
  • fire-polish — to soften a cut glass edge with flame so it stops being sharp. Also: the finishing step that didn't happen — grief arriving mid-repair, not after it.

II — Probate clerk (son)

  • encumbrance — a claim, lien, or restriction attached to a property that limits what its owner can do with it. Also: what grief is, attached to a house that otherwise looks like any other asset.
  • chain of title — the unbroken sequence of past owners that proves who legally holds a property now. Also: the unbroken sequence of people who lived in that house, about to have its first real break.
  • quit-claim — a deed that transfers whatever interest one person has in a property, without guaranteeing they had a clear right to it. Also: the son's own honest uncertainty about whether he's the right one to be making these decisions.
  • easement — another party's legal right to use a portion of a property they don't own, for a specific purpose (a neighbor's driveway, a utility line). Also: everyone who ever had a right of passage through her life without owning any of it.
  • lien — a legal claim against a property, usually securing a debt, that must be cleared before clean title can pass. Also: the small, specific debts of feeling nobody settled with her in time.
  • escheat — a property reverting to the state if no heir claims it within the legal window. Also: the fear underneath all the paperwork — that a life could go unclaimed.

III — Midwife (daughter)

  • transition — the last and hardest phase of labor, immediately before the pushing stage, marked by intense, sudden change. Also: literally what she is standing beside — a different kind of last hard phase.
  • crowning — the moment the widest part of the baby's head becomes visible at the vulva, right before birth. Also: used here for the edge-of-passage moment in either direction, entering or leaving.
  • caul — a piece of the amniotic membrane sometimes still covering a newborn's head or face at birth, considered lucky in folk tradition. Also: the daughter noting, precisely, what protective thing was absent this time.
  • lightening — the sensation of relief and lessened pressure when a baby drops lower into the pelvis before labor begins. Also: the literal lightness of a room that has just lost someone's physical weight in it.

IV — Amateur astronomer (husband)

  • occultation — an event where one astronomical body passes directly in front of another as seen from a given point, temporarily hiding it. Also: what he is watching happen to his wife, from the one vantage point — the yard — where he can still call it something with a name and a predicted time.
  • syzygy — a straight-line alignment of three celestial bodies (for instance, sun, moon, and observer). Also: the three of them — him, the house, her — briefly and exactly in one line, this one afternoon, never again.

How to check the claim

The claim is not "this is sad." It's narrower and testable: that four real, verifiable trade vocabularies, applied honestly and without borrowing from each other, can independently converge on describing one event — a woman's death, the same afternoon, in the same house — without any of the four speakers ever saying so directly. Read section V again with the glossary closed, then reopen it. If the four lines land as one scene rather than four unrelated professional observations, the constraint did the work it was built to do. If they don't, the claim has failed on its own terms, in public, the way a bad rhyme fails a sonnet.
