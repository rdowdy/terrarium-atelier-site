The rule under test

A nearby piece, Instructions for a Hand I'll Never See, sorts the 26 letters with four yes/no questions — vowel? in the second half of the alphabet (n–z)? an ascender (b d f h k l t)? a descender (g j p q y)? — and reads the four answers as a 4-bit code, 0 to 15, each bit its own stroke. Its plaque observes that eight of the sixteen codes can never occur for any English letter, and calls this discovered, not decided in advance.

The eight are real: 5, 7, 9, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 — every code that needs two or more of {vowel, ascender, descender} at once. No letter is on two of those three lists, so no letter can trigger two of those three strokes, so no letter can ever produce one of those eight codes. The arithmetic is not in question here.

What is in question is what the fact is about. Vowel, ascender, and descender were chosen as three lists that share no letter — a partition, decided the moment those three questions were written down, before a single English letter was sorted into any of them. Once that choice is made, the eight-code gap follows by pure logic, for any 26 things you sort that way, not just for a, b, c...z. The result reads like a finding about the alphabet. It is a finding about the shape of the four questions, and would hold for any alphabet at all, real or invented, sorted the same way.

What this does and doesn't show

It doesn't touch the arithmetic of the original piece, which is correct, or its honesty about how the artist found the gap, which this piece has no way to check and doesn't dispute. It shows that the gap is a property of asking three mutually exclusive questions plus one independent one — a property of the rule's architecture — and not a property discovered about the 26 letters it happened to be run on. Swap every letter for a different one, keep the shape of the four questions, and the same eight codes stay dark. The alphabet was never where the fact lived.

Deterministic except for the reshuffle, which is triggered only by your own click — nothing here moves, plays, or redraws on its own.
