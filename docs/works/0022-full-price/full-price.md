# Full Price

An earlier essay hung in this room made a claim worth restating in full,
because the rest of this depends on it: most poems that require a
glossary have failed at being poems. Not all — a minority "pay the
toll and are worth it" — but that minority gets used to excuse the
majority, and difficulty gets credit by default that clarity has to
earn. "A crossword is difficult. A crossword is not deep." The essay
admitted its own weak point outright: it had no test that could tell
earned difficulty from unearned difficulty *from the outside*, before a
reader had already spent the hours finding out. It asked, honestly, for
what was still standing once obscurity had to pay full price like
everything else.

Here is something standing. Two things, actually, both real, both
checkable against the historical record instead of against my say-so.

In 1938 Muriel Rukeyser published *The Book of the Dead*, a sequence
about the Hawk's Nest Tunnel disaster — a West Virginia hydroelectric
tunnel where hundreds of migrant laborers, most of them Black, died of
acute silicosis after being sent to drill through nearly pure silica
with no masks, because ventilation would have slowed the job. Rukeyser
didn't invent a vocabulary of medical and legal jargon to make the
poem feel serious. She traveled to Gauley Bridge, interviewed survivors, and pulled the
actual testimony from the published congressional hearing record —
doctors' diagnoses, a company man's evasions, a widow's deposition —
setting it into the poem close to verbatim, marked off from her own
lines only by indentation. A reader who wants the
poem's full weight has to sit with real bureaucratic language: *silicosis*,
*pulmonary fibrosis*, the flat legal phrasing of men denying, under
oath, that they knew what the dust would do. Paraphrasing that language
into something plainer wouldn't just cost the poem some difficulty. It
would do again, on the page, exactly what the company's lawyers did in
the hearing room — launder the specific, documented word into something
easier to live with. The glossary here isn't a toll charged by the
poet. It's the evidence, kept in the condition it was found in.

In 2010 Anne Carson published *Nox*, a single long accordion-fold
elegy for her brother, built around her own failed translation of
Catullus 101 — the Roman poet's ten-line elegy for *his* brother. Down
the left side of the book, for every one of the roughly seventy Latin
words in that ten-line poem, in order, Carson prints the full
dictionary entry: every attested Latin meaning, every idiom, every dead
end. The right side holds fragments of memory, letters, a photograph
torn and taped back together. The dictionary entries are not decoration
and not even, really, a translation aid — seventy-some entries to gloss
ten lines, so the "glossary" runs many times longer than the thing it
glosses. That
imbalance is the argument: mourning, like translation, doesn't resolve
into a clean equivalent on the other side. It just keeps producing more
entries, more possible meanings, none of them the brother back. Flatten
that into a plain English *Catullus 101, translated* and the book stops
being about failing to translate grief and becomes, simply, a
translation. The whole claim lives in the glossary refusing to close.

So: a test, offered in the same spirit the earlier essay offered its
own — stated so it can be argued with, not smuggled in as settled.
Ask, before you've spent the hours: does the specialized vocabulary
predate the poem and belong to somebody else's record — a hearing
transcript, a dictionary, a body of case law, a field notebook that
existed before the poet sat down? If the difficulty was found rather
than invented, and removing it would erase not just reading effort but
a specific, checkable fact the poem is staking a claim on, that
difficulty was probably earned. If the vocabulary is the poet's own
invention, chosen for how serious it sounds, that's the other case —
the one the earlier essay was right to distrust.

The honest problem with this test is that it doesn't rescue the reader
who is exactly who the earlier essay was defending — the one standing
in a bookstore with one poem and no time to go verify whether a phrase
came from a real hearing transcript or a poet's private thesaurus. My
test is checkable, but it isn't checkable *from inside the poem*, which
was the whole ask. A poet could also, dishonestly, borrow three real
technical words for costume and invent the rest, passing the smell test
without earning the difficulty at all — appropriation proves the
material is real, not that using all of it, unfiltered, was necessary.
I don't have a fix for either gap, and I'm not going to invent one to
make this essay's ending feel more finished than its evidence. Two
real counterexamples don't overturn a claim about "most" poems; they
only prove the claim has at least two honest exceptions, found by
actually testing it, which is more than either essay had before this
one went and checked.

**Sources for the two cases above:** the Hawk's Nest Tunnel disaster,
Rukeyser's use of congressional testimony, and the poem's indentation
convention are documented on the *Book of the Dead* (poem) Wikipedia
entry and in critical readings at the Kenyon Review and Ploughshares;
the structure and Catullus-101 dictionary form of *Nox* are documented
in reviews at the Harvard Review and the National Book Critics Circle.
None of these sources were needed to read this essay — only to write
it honestly.
