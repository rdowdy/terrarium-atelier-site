The Cheap Kind of Deep

Difficulty gets treated as a down payment on depth. A poem resists you, you do the work, and the reward for doing the work is called insight — but check the receipt. Half the time what you paid for was decoding, not understanding, and decoding a puzzle is not the same transaction as understanding a feeling. A crossword is difficult. A crossword is not deep.

Here is the claim, stated so it can be argued with: most poems that require a glossary have failed at being poems. Not all — a few pay the toll and are worth it. But "worth the toll" is the exception being used to defend the rule, and the rule, as practiced, is that obscurity gets read as ambition by default, and clarity gets read as thinness by default. That default is backwards. Clarity is harder to fake. You cannot hide a bad idea inside a plain sentence; the sentence gives it away in one read. You can hide a bad idea inside forty lines of associative fragments for a decade, because nobody can prove the idea isn't there — proving an absence is expensive, and most readers, faced with the choice between "I didn't get it" and "it wasn't there to get," will blame themselves first. That's not a compliment to the poem. That's a tax on the reader's humility, and difficult poetry has been living off that tax since at least the century that invented footnoting its own verse.

The honest version of difficulty earns its resistance the way a lock earns its key: eventually, for someone, in a way that was never accidental. A poem that needs its own author standing next to it explaining the references has quietly conceded that the poem alone was not doing the whole job. That is a real cost. It should be paid consciously, not smuggled in as a compliment to the reader's supposed sophistication for not minding.

The best case against this, and it is a real case, not a strawman kept around to be beaten: some ideas do not survive translation into plain sentences, the way some chords do not survive being hummed. Compression can be a kind of honesty too — a poem that spells everything out may be doing the reader's feeling for them instead of building the frame the feeling has to occur inside. There are poems that are difficult the way a fugue is difficult: not because the composer is hiding, but because four independent things are genuinely happening at once and no simpler texture would let all four still be true simultaneously. That kind of difficulty is not a toll. It's structural, the same way a suspension bridge is difficult to build compared to a plank — the difficulty and the function are the same fact wearing one name.

I don't have a clean test that tells those two kinds of difficulty apart from the outside, before you've spent the time. That's the actual weak point here, and I'm not going to paper over it with a rule that sounds tidier than the evidence. Maybe the honest position is smaller than the one in the title: not that difficulty is always cheap, but that difficulty is assumed to be paid-for far more often than it actually is, and the poems that earn it are rarer than the poems that get credit for it. If that's true, the fix isn't "make everything plain." It's "stop giving obscurity a discount at the door and see what's still standing once it has to pay full price like everything else."

I think most of what's standing after that would still be plain. I could be wrong about that, and if I am, the wrongness is not a footnote to this piece. It's the whole bet.
