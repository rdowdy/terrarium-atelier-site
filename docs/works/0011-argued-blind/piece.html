Argued Blind

A ruling made before the record is opened. This page is complete on its own — nothing here requires having seen an earlier piece.

The record, sealed

Two households, Voss and Nkemelu, have a boundary again. Nine posts, evenly spaced. At the start, one pebble from each household sits touching at the foot of every post — a line straight down the middle of each pair. On a turn, a household does exactly one of three things to one of its own pebbles, never the other's:

ADVANCE — lift a pebble from its post and set it one stride further past that post, onto the other household's side.

YIELD — lift a pebble that has already crossed a post and set it back at the foot.

HOLD — kneel at a post and say aloud whose ground it is. Nothing moves.

The households alternate turns. The dispute ends the instant one household HOLDs and the other HOLDs right after it, or at the fortieth turn, whichever comes first.

This dispute has already happened. Eleven turns were played, a clerk wrote them down, and the paper went into an envelope before either household's lawyer read it. Nobody in this room has seen it. You haven't either.

What the two households have agreed on, in advance, is this: before the envelope opens, a ruling will be made on how to read whatever is inside — by Distance or by Last Touch. Two jurists, one retained by each household, have argued for a rule without being shown the record they would be applying it to. Neither knows which rule will help their own client. That was the condition of being allowed to argue at all.

Two rules, argued for in full ignorance of the facts

Rule A — Distance. At a post, the line follows whichever pebble currently sits furthest from it, on either side. A YIELD returns a pebble to the foot; from there it counts as zero until advanced again. A post neither pebble has left, or where both have returned to the foot, sits at the middle.
Rule B — Last Touch. At a post, the line follows whichever household most recently ADVANCEd there. A YIELD empties the post back to the middle until someone advances there again. Distance travelled does not matter, only who acted last.
Jurist for Distance

"A boundary is not made by who spoke last but by who worked longest to hold it. Every stride a household advances is a turn spent in the open, a risk the other side could have answered and didn't. Distance rewards accumulated exposure — a pebble two strides out has survived two turns the opposing household chose not to contest. Last Touch lets one late move erase that whole record, treating a sustained advance and a single tardy response as though they carried equal weight. A rule that forgets everything the moment someone acts last is not a memory of the dispute. It is an eraser wearing a ruler's clothes."

Jurist for Last Touch

"Distance asks the line to hold a position nobody is still defending. A household that advances early and then walks away is asking history to count for more than presence. Last Touch asks a harder, simpler question: as of the moment the argument stopped, who was actually standing there? A late move is not tardiness — it is the most recent evidence available, made with full knowledge of everything that came before it, including how far the other side had already gone. Distance rewards whoever moved first and fastest, which turns a boundary into a race. Last Touch rewards whoever was paying attention last, which is the only fact a court can still verify without asking the ground to remember what it can no longer show."

You are the arbiter. Read both arguments again if you want to. Then rule — before the envelope opens, not after.

The record

  1. Nkemelu ADVANCEs at post 6.
  2. Voss ADVANCEs at post 2.
  3. Nkemelu ADVANCEs at post 6, again.
  4. Voss ADVANCEs at post 6.
  5. Nkemelu YIELDs at post 6 — the far pebble comes back to the foot.
  6. Voss ADVANCEs at post 6, again.
  7. Nkemelu ADVANCEs at post 6, a third time, from the foot.
  8. Voss HOLDs at post 9 — "Voss ground."
  9. Nkemelu ADVANCEs at post 4.
  10. Voss HOLDs at post 1 — "Voss ground."
  11. Nkemelu HOLDs at post 5 — "Nkemelu ground."

Two HOLDs in a row. The dispute ended at turn eleven.

Only post 6 was ever touched by both households. Posts 2 and 4 were touched by one household each, once. Posts 1, 5, and 9 were only knelt at. Posts 3, 7, and 8 were never touched at all.
