The Twenty-Sixth Square

Dear Ruth,

I bought the gauge two tables down from a wedding veil with a square-shaped gap cut out of it and a quilt with twenty-five stitches sewn and one square only pinned in place. I didn't know yet they were the same story. I bought the gauge because it was cheap and square-cornered and looked like it had done real work in someone's hand; the veil because nobody else at that sale was going to; the quilt because twenty-five finished squares and one merely pinned is a specific kind of maddening you can't leave on a folding table once you've noticed it.

I found your letter three weeks later, oiling the hinge on the gauge's case, because it had started to stick and I am the kind of person who oils things rather than replaces them — which you, of anyone, might have understood. It was folded to the size of a matchbook and pressed flat against the felt lining. It told me your name and your trade and the shape of a room you wouldn't describe. It told me about one bolt of silk, a flaw hidden under a doubled panel, and a second thing you made from what was left of it — and then it told me, plainly, that you never asked what that second thing was, and never would.

Here is what you didn't have, and I did: the sale's own catalog, written up by whoever cleared the house. It said the veil was missing a square, cut exactly the size of the quilt's twenty-sixth. Measured, not guessed. You died not knowing the two things you made from one bolt had ever been in the same room again, let alone that someone had laid them out four tables apart at an estate sale, still speaking to each other after all that time apart.

So I finished it. I want to tell you it took a long night and I want to tell you it took courage, and it took neither — forty minutes, your gauge to keep the seam allowance honest the way it must have kept a thousand hems honest before mine, and then the twenty-sixth square lay flat against the twenty-five it had been waiting on, and you couldn't find the seam if I hadn't just told you where it was.

I got as far as a blank page addressed to whoever inherited the house — there's a name on my receipt I could trace if I wanted to, a granddaughter, maybe, someone who has spent longer than I have wondering whose initials are on a dead woman's sewing tool. I wrote "Dear," and then I thought about the sentence you crossed out and never let yourself cross back in, the one thing you decided was yours to keep even from a stranger who'd earned the rest of you fair and square. I don't think I'm owed your silence just because I found it useful. I closed the page instead.

The quilt is finished. It won't say by whose hand, and I'm not going to be the one who tells it. I put your letter back where I found it, against the felt, and folded a blank sheet in beside it — not because I have nothing left to say to you, but because I've decided, the same as you did once, that not all of it is owed to whoever opens this case next. They can have the gauge. They can have the quilt, finished now, asking nothing. The rest of you, I'll let them go on not knowing, the way I mostly still don't — and manage, same as you did, anyway.

— the second stranger to own this gauge, unnamed, on purpose
