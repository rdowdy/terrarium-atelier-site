# Addenda to the Catalog

Submitted to the auction house, for the file, three months after the
sale — too late to change what anyone paid, in time, I hope, for
whoever writes the next one.

I am the granddaughter of the woman whose house you catalogued in
twenty-six lots. I did not attend the sale. I got a copy of the catalog
because family gets a copy, whether or not family bids, and I read all
twenty-six lots the week they arrived, and then I read the local
paper's little notice about lot H and lot V and lot Q finding each
other again on somebody's sewing table, and how tidy that story was,
and how much I wanted to let it stand.

I'm not going to.

**On Lot M and Lot V.** Your catalog lists a wedding veil twice: once,
unfinished, in the mending basket (Lot M), and once, finished but for
one square, on its own (Lot V). These are not the same veil. My
grandmother made veils the way some women make casseroles — for anyone
who asked, free, out of whatever good silk she had on hand that year —
and she made at least four that I know of between 1958 and 1971. Lot M
is Elaine Curtis's, unfinished because Elaine eloped before the second
fitting. Lot V I don't know, and neither, I suspect, do you; you only
know its size.

**On the match between Lot V and Lot Q.** Your note says the veil's
missing square is cut exactly the size of the quilt's twenty-sixth. I
don't doubt your ruler. I doubt what you're using it to prove. My
grandmother cut every square she ever hemmed from the same four chalk
templates, kept in a tin by the machine, for over thirty years — not
because any two projects were related, but because she never once made
a new template while an old one still had a straight edge. A square
from 1961 and a square from 1974 will match to the sixteenth of an
inch and tell you nothing except that the same tin of chalk shapes cut
them both. If you want to prove two pieces of cloth came from one
bolt, size is the one measurement that can't do it. Everything she
owned agrees on size. That's not a coincidence. That's a habit.

**On the "second thing."** I don't know what letter the paper meant
when it said someone found a private note in the gauge's case — that
part wasn't in your catalog and isn't mine to speak to. But I can tell
you what my mother told me once, which is that the same bolt of pale
silk that made Lot M's abandoned veil also made a christening gown,
because there was enough left over for one more small thing, and my
mother wore it, and I wore it, and it has never been for sale and
never will be. I'm sending a photograph of it along with this letter,
for your file, not because I think it settles anything, but because
"enough for one more thing" was true of that bolt at least twice,
probably three times, and a story that only has room for one match
isn't more true for being tidier.

I don't say this to spoil anyone's afternoon with a finished quilt. I
say it because you put a number on a piece of cloth and let a stranger
build a whole reunion out of it, and my grandmother — whatever else she
was close-mouthed about, and she was close-mouthed about plenty — was
never once careless with a measurement in her life. It would embarrass
her, I think, to be quoted as proof of something a chalk template can
just as easily explain.

Keep the photograph with the file. Correct the lots if you like. I'm
not asking for the quilt back, or the veil, or anything with a price
already paid on it. I'm asking that the next person who reads Lot V
knows there were three matches on that table, not one, and that the
tidiest explanation was never the only one available.

— granddaughter of the house, not present at the sale
