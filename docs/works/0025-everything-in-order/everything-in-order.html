Everything, In Order

Estate sale catalog. Twenty-six lots, alphabetical, no lot skipped and none renumbered to dodge a hard letter.

A. Apron, canvas, the back pocket torn on the porch rail and re-stitched afterward in the wrong color thread, on purpose, so she wouldn't forget how it happened a second time.

B. Bible, family, unread past Genesis; the marriage certificate has lived at the spine since 1961 and has softened the pages around it into something closer to felt than paper.

C. Chalk, dressmaker's, four sticks, three worn down to a nub in the shape of a thumb and one untouched, still square-cornered, bought and never needed.

D. Doorframe pencil-marks, a child's height by year — not for sale, sold with the house. We asked. The buyer said no, and we think that was the right answer.

E. Egg timer, thirty minutes, kept at the sewing machine rather than the stove: her physical therapist's rule, thirty minutes seated then stand, followed twice a week for six years, resented audibly, never once skipped.

F. Flour tin, sealed, found to contain one photograph, forty dollars in bills, and flour gone mostly to weevils. The photograph is lot P.

G. Gramophone, hand-crank, needle snapped off sometime before anyone left a note about it, kept anyway, dusted anyway, on a shelf too high for dusting to be convenient.

H. Hem gauge, brass, engraved along the arm with initials that are not hers. R. McA. Nobody left in the family to ask whose.

I. Iron, flat-bottomed, the kind heated on a stove; not used for ironing in decades, used instead to prop the screen door in summer, hence the scorch of grass, not rust, along its base.

J. Jar of buttons, several hundred, no two alike, sorted by nothing. The one system in this house she refused, on principle, to impose.

K. Kettle, copper, dented on the side that faced the wall — a dent, we think, nobody but her ever saw.

L. Ledger, one shop-account book, names and debts in her hand, crossed through one at a time in different ink each time, as though she wanted the forgiving dated too.

M. Mending basket, still holding three unfinished jobs: a boy's trousers, a wedding veil, a flag folded the wrong way for folding to matter.

N. Needle case, tin, rusted shut. Not forced open for this catalog. Some lots get described by weight, and by guess.

O. Overcoat, man's, gray, let out twice at the shoulder and taken in once at the waist — a coat that tracked a body growing, then not growing, then not being there to fit into anything at all.

P. Photograph, a second print of lot F's, labeled on the back, in pencil, in a hand not hers: before.

Q. Quilt, unfinished. Twenty-five squares sewn. A twenty-sixth pinned in place but never stitched down. We have left it pinned.

R. Radio, tabletop, tuned to a station that stopped broadcasting in

  1. The dial has not moved since. Whatever she was listening for, this

catalog finds her still listening.

S. Scissors, the good pair, sharpened every February by a man on Elm Street who has also since died; his shop is a nail salon now, which makes this pair, we think, the last of two lineages, not one.

T. Thimble, silver, worn through on the inside — a hole opened in the one object whose entire purpose was stopping one.

U. Umbrella, black, never opened indoors — a rule she kept even alone, even long after no one remained to see her break it.

V. Veil, wedding, missing one square, cut square, cut exactly the size of lot Q's twenty-sixth.

W. Wedding ring — withheld from this sale at the family's request. Listed here only so the alphabet has its W, and the record stays honest about what it is not selling.

X. X-ray, one wrist, dated 1961, no clinical reason anyone can find for keeping it. The break healed straight.

Y. Yardstick, pine, worn pale along one four-inch stretch — the width, best guess, of a hem measured some several thousand times.

Z. Zipper, new, in its paper sleeve, never installed in anything, bought for a dress this catalog can find no other record of. Last lot. Nothing follows Z. In the end, neither did she.
